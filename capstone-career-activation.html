Structuring the business context

What the panel needs first

The market, the ICP, and the problem worth solving — stated in one or two sentences before any tactic appears. A panel that doesn't understand the "why" will judge every later slide against the wrong bar.

The trap to avoid

Opening with tools and channels instead of the business problem. Lead with the decision you were trying to move, then show the system built to move it.

Presenting the AI-first architecture

Show the system, not the stack

A slide listing every tool used is a resume, not an architecture. Show how ICP research, content generation, distribution, and measurement agents hand off to each other — and where a human sits in the loop.

Name the guardrails

A panel of practitioners will ask what happens when an agent is wrong. Have the answer ready: what each agent could decide autonomously, and what always required review before it shipped.

Walking through the customer journey

One journey, fully told

Pick a single representative customer path from first touch to conversion (or retention) and narrate it stage by stage, rather than presenting every segment and channel combination at once.

Where AI actually changed the journey

Point to the specific moments personalization, scoring, or an agent's decision changed what the customer experienced — not just where AI was involved in production.

Telling the media & measurement story

Evidence over assertion

"CTR improved" is an assertion. A measurement plan, a before/after chart with the confidence interval, and a note on what didn't move is evidence — and evidence is what survives a skeptical panel.

Own the limits

Naming a test that was inconclusive, a channel that underperformed, or a metric that's still noisy builds more credibility than a slide where everything went perfectly.

What a strong capstone has in common

1. A business problem stated before any tactic
2. One journey told in full instead of every segment sketched thinly
3. A named guardrail for every AI agent in the system
4. A measurement plan built before the campaign, not backfilled after
5. At least one honestly reported result that didn't work
6. A clear statement of what the presenter personally built vs. what the team built

Anatomy of an industry panel

Who's usually in the room

Practicing marketers, hiring managers, and founders — people evaluating whether they'd trust this work in production, not academics grading a rubric.

What they're actually scoring

Judgment under ambiguity: could this person make a reasonable call when the data was incomplete and the deadline was real? The polish of the deck matters far less than the quality of the decisions behind it.

Questions a panel will ask

QuestionWhat they're testing
"What would you do differently with another month?"Self-awareness and prioritization under real constraints
"How do you know the AI-generated output was any good?"Whether a human review process actually existed, or was assumed
"What's the one number that mattered most here?"Whether the presenter can distinguish signal from vanity metrics
"Who owns this if it breaks in production?"Operational thinking beyond the demo

Receiving critique gracefully

Reframe it as signal

A sharp question from the panel is free market research on how the work reads to someone who wasn't in the room while it was built. Treat it that way, not as an attack to survive.

The one move that backfires

Defending a weak decision instead of naming the tradeoff that led to it. "Given the timeline, I prioritized X over Y" reads as judgment; arguing the critique is wrong reads as the opposite.

From capstone to portfolio

The deck, cleaned up

Trimmed to the story that survived the panel's questions — not the version built the night before presenting.

One artifact per system

A screenshot of the measurement dashboard, the agent architecture diagram, or a sample of the AI-generated content with the human edit shown.

The result, with its context

The number, the baseline it's measured against, and the timeframe — never a bare percentage with no comparison point.

The critique, addressed

A short note on what the panel pushed back on and what would change next time — proof the feedback landed.

Writing the case study

Structure it as Problem → Approach → Result

The business problem in one line, the specific system or decision built to address it, and the measured outcome — in that order, every time, so a reader can skim it in thirty seconds.

Write for a skimming hiring manager

Bold the one number that matters, cut the tool inventory, and let the deck link carry the detail a resume line can't.

Mapping your capstone to a role

Work backward from the job post

Pull the three or four responsibilities a target posting repeats most, then find the capstone artifact that maps to each — not every artifact you built.

One role, one framing

The same capstone can lead with the CRM architecture for a RevOps application and lead with the creative testing program for a performance role — same work, different emphasis.

A 90-day plan built from the panel's actual critique is worth more than a generic list of courses — it's evidence you took the feedback and acted on it.

What goes in each phase

Days 1–30: close the named gaps

Take the panel's specific critique — a weak measurement plan, a shallow guardrail story — and go deep on exactly that, not a broad refresh of everything.

Days 31–90: build, then activate

A second, smaller artifact that demonstrates the closed gap, followed by applications, informational interviews, and outreach to people met at the panel while the work is still fresh.

Networking after the panel

Follow up within 48 hours

A short, specific note referencing the actual question a panelist asked — not a generic connection request — while the presentation is still memorable to them.

Stay in the room

Panelists are hiring managers, referral sources, and future collaborators before they're anything else. A panel is a room full of warm contacts, not a one-time evaluation.

Interview storytelling

The capstone is your STAR bank

Every interview behavioral question — a conflict, a tight deadline, a failure — almost certainly has a real answer inside the capstone project if it's been lived through once already.

Lead with the decision, not the tool

"I prioritized the CRM cleanup over a new channel because the data was untrustworthy" demonstrates judgment. Listing the tools used demonstrates exposure.

Best practices

1. Open with the business problem, never the tool stack
2. Tell one customer journey completely instead of many thinly
3. Name the guardrail for every AI agent in the system
4. Report at least one honest result that didn't work
5. Treat panel critique as free research, not an attack to defend against
6. Cut the portfolio deck to what survived the panel's questions
7. Reframe the same artifacts differently for each target role
8. Build the 90-day plan from the panel's specific critique, not a generic list
9. Follow up with panelists within 48 hours, referencing their actual question
10. Keep the capstone as your STAR-answer bank for every interview that follows
